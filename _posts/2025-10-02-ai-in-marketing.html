Personalization is a ranking and causal decision problem, not simply a prediction of who will click. A production system must retrieve candidates, rank a slate under constraints, estimate incremental impact, and learn without confusing exposure with preference.

System architecture

A common design has two stages. Candidate generation uses approximate nearest-neighbor retrieval, co-visitation, popularity, or business rules to produce a few hundred feasible items. A ranker then scores candidates using user, item, context, recency, price, inventory, and policy features. The final slate applies diversity, eligibility, frequency, and safety constraints.

candidates = retrieve(user_embedding, item_index, k=500)
scores = rank(user, candidates, context)
slate = constraints.select(scores, diversity, inventory, policy)

Embeddings capture collaborative and semantic relationships, but they should not override hard constraints. An unavailable, restricted, or already-purchased item should be removed by policy rather than left to the model's probability.

Ranking objectives

Pointwise models estimate a response probability. Pairwise methods such as Bayesian Personalized Ranking optimize that a positive item outranks a negative one. Listwise losses optimize the ordering of a slate. Offline metrics include recall@k, MRR, and NDCG, but the useful metric depends on the action: watch time, qualified conversion, retention, margin, or long-term value.

Train with time-based and user-aware splits. Repeated interactions from the same user can leak identity and make a model appear to generalize. Negative sampling should reflect the items users could actually have seen, otherwise the model learns an unrealistic task.

Exposure bias

Click logs are generated by the previous policy. An item cannot be clicked if it was never displayed, so observed preference is confounded by exposure. Propensity scoring weights an interaction by the probability that the old policy displayed it. Randomized exploration provides cleaner data but must be bounded by safety and business constraints.

Counterfactual evaluation is high variance when propensities are small. Clip extreme weights, report effective sample size, and compare against replayed baselines. Do not launch a new ranker based only on an offline gain that comes from unsupported counterfactuals.

Uplift modeling

Response prediction asks whether a user will convert. Uplift asks whether the treatment caused an incremental change:

uplift(x) = E[Y | treatment, x] - E[Y | control, x]

Uplift can avoid spending incentives on users who would convert anyway and avoid targeting users who are harmed by a treatment. Estimate it with randomized treatment/control data or causal methods with explicit assumptions. Validate incremental revenue, not only model AUC.

Experimentation

Randomize at a stable user or household level, define a primary metric before looking at results, and account for interference between recommendation surfaces. Monitor short-term clicks and delayed outcomes such as retention, complaints, unsubscribes, and margin. A ranking system is successful when it improves the user's long-term experience, not when it maximizes one easily manipulated event.
